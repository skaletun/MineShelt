# Form and proportion — the long-distance test

For the **relief of buildings themselves** (separate from terrain relief), consult [architectural-depth-and-relief.md](architectural-depth-and-relief.md): wall sections, true volumes, openings, projecting frames and multi-angle proof.

The building must communicate its role before the viewer recognizes individual blocks. Analyze it as silhouettes, masses, visible load paths and rhythm. This reference is about making **specific architectural choices**, not enforcing a universal ratio.

## Start with the hero view

Establish where the building is first seen: trail bend, village plaza, riverbank, valley floor, road or cliff. From that view, ask:
- What is the primary outline against sky/land?
- Can the viewer identify the entry or front side?
- Where does the eye settle? A forge chimney, lantern room, tower, porch or roof apex should be a deliberate focal point.
- Is the outline legible without trapdoors, buttons, fences and plants?
- Do changes in height explain different internal uses?

If the answer is negative, change the **mass**, not the decoration.

## Plan on Minecraft's block grid

Record X/Z footprint, elevation datum and total height. Distinguish playable area from outside dimensions: a 9-by-9 facade with 2-block-thick walls does not contain a 9-by-9 room. Account for beams, corners, stairs, door clearances, roof thickness, smoke passage and chimney space.

A useful early sketch includes:
1. A primary enclosed volume.
2. A subordinate volume if the role warrants it.
3. A roof covering each volume and a believable junction between them.
4. An entrance and approach axis.
5. A tall or unique focal component only if justified.
6. Ground contact and grade changes.

Test at least two meaningful massing directions, such as narrow/tall versus low/sprawling, then select one against the brief. Do not proliferate meaningless design alternatives.

## Mass hierarchy, not uniform boxes

Create one dominant volume and one or a few supporting forms. A village baker might comprise a large hot-work room, a low fuel lean-to, a slightly taller smoke hood and a front sales porch. Each shape comes from use. A fortified gatehouse instead derives its hierarchy from passage, defense, height and sightline.

Typical failure: a flat cuboid receives 20 fence posts and 30 trapdoors and is called “detailed”. The problem was the cuboid. Change its section, roof, fenestration, projections or relation to the terrain.

### Human scale

Minecraft's unit is a block, but gameplay entities, doors and stairs impose practical constraints. Do not treat approximate medieval real-world dimensions as literal law: tune proportions in-world.

- 1-block doors and 2-block-high passages may function in vanilla conventions, but check collisions, equipment, intended mobs and accessibility.
- 3-block-high rooms are compact and risk heavy ceilings once beams/lamps are added. Larger workspaces need more headroom.
- A staircase that looks plausible on a plan may consume many blocks in depth; walk it before allocating upper rooms.
- Corridors, defensive passages, balcony widths and ramps need clear usable widths after supports and furniture.
- Roof pitch should match climate, span, materials and silhouette. A uniformly steep roof on every building loses cultural specificity.

Use dimensions as constraints, not immutable style recipes.

## Proportion reading and rhythm

Examine relationships:
- solid wall to window void;
- height to footprint;
- tower shaft to cap;
- support size to apparent load;
- roof thickness to span;
- overhang to facade height;
- frequency of facade bays to actual room layout.

Repetition can be intentional; so can interruption. A workshop may repeat structural bays and widen a single bay at its loading door. Asymmetry is powerful when explained by site access, extensions, wear, occupation or utility.

Avoid mechanical window spacing when interior partitions differ. Avoid “perfectly imperfect” alternating patterns; they are conspicuous because the designer has no reason for the variation.

## Section beats facade

Think in cross-section before decoration:
- Can the roof meet the wall without nonsensical floating trim?
- Is the attic tall enough for its intended use?
- Does a second story have a sensible floor depth, stair entry and window levels?
- Do exposed beams connect to supports?
- Does the foundation actually adapt to the site?

For a building on a slope, define threshold elevation and stepped foundation heights. Do not silently carve a giant level square and then place a floating visual shell above it.

## Deliberate asymmetry

Examples of *motivated* deviation:
- a wet-weather covered side door on the path from a fishing dock;
- taller northern wall protecting work from severe coastal wind;
- chimney shifted towards a working hearth;
- later repair in a different stone or timber generation;
- an annex pressed against the uphill side because downhill excavation is expensive.

Examples of *unmotivated* deviation:
- randomly shifting every second beam;
- three distinct roof pitches on a hut without useful plan changes;
- adding balconies with no door access;
- staggering walls just to break symmetry.

## Distance checks

Observe at three scales:
- **Far** (landmark): profile, focal hierarchy, geographic fit.
- **Middle** (composition): masses, roof relationships, door placement, material balance.
- **Near** (craft): supports, joinery suggestions, block states, believable wear.

A build that passes the near test but fails far or middle is not complete.

## Editing order

When proportions fail:
1. Resolve site and base elevation.
2. Adjust the roof profile and key mass ratios.
3. Shift major openings and volumes.
4. Rebalance materials.
5. Only then restore necessary detail.

This order prevents cosmetic patches from hiding structural problems.
