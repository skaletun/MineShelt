# Human builder apprenticeship — how to think, act and improve

This document operationalizes a human craftsperson's practice. The target is not “indistinguishable from a human” as a detector claim, but **visible architectural competence, site awareness and iterative manual judgment**.

## Build as a cycle, not one-shot production

A practiced builder switches constantly between **seeing, imagining, placing, stepping away, correcting**. The biggest difference from generic AI output is not an enormous library of ornate details: it is deliberate negative space, comparison of alternatives and willingness to rebuild a weak section.

### The seven connected studios

1. **Site studio**: survey with coordinates, views, hazards and cultural/ecological context.
2. **Concept studio**: purpose, historical construction story, silhouette and one focal point.
3. **Palette studio**: art-directed material swatches, lighting, surface transitions.
4. **Terrain studio**: authored slopes, cliffs, watercourses, paths and ground contact.
5. **Structure studio**: mass, foundation, roof junction, window/door rhythms.
6. **Life studio**: routes, rooms, furniture, utilities, planting and wear.
7. **Critique studio**: compare in-world screens, identify root cause and make a real repair.

Terrain and structure interact: terrain shouldn't always be done entirely before foundation, nor always added only after the house. In a steep site, alternate terrain profile and foundation decisions until both fit.

## Choose a lead feature

A professional scene typically has a *lead* and supporting elements:
- a tower with a quiet ridge and path;
- an old mill with a river/waterwheel; 
- a cathedral with street approach and courtyard;
- a mountain shrine beneath one dramatic cliff.

An exuberant cliff, giant tree, complex roof and intricate ground gradient all competing at equal strength is a warning sign. Edit the composition: declare which should dominate, reduce the others.

## Decision log at every phase

Instead of producing a long generic checklist, state **decision, evidence, rejected alternative and expected visual effect**. Example:

> Decision: recess the forge entry on the southeast wall by two blocks. Evidence: village path arrives from that direction and snow accumulates on the northern windward face. Rejected: a centered exposed door; it made the threshold feel unprotected. Check: entering players have a clear two-block-wide approach and the south elevation retains a readable focal point.

Numbers in this example are illustrative; measure actual world geometry before a real placement.

A short, specific log is far more valuable than 100 lines saying “adding details”.

## Three visual scales and two presentation modes

Inspect:
- far distance: silhouette, negative space, terrain mass and skyline;
- middle: mass and facade relationships, landscape routes, cluster rhythm;
- close: texture families, joints, collisions, plant/root/roof details.

Also inspect in **raw vanilla-like view** where feasible and the intended showcase renderer/resource pack. Shaders can hide defects through bloom/shadows. The deliverable should not depend on a single camera angle to look finished.

## Skill drills (repeatable evaluations)

### Drill A: one-palette two-house contrast
Using the same six or fewer families of vanilla blocks, design a fisherman's hut and mountain cartographer's workshop. They should have different identities through floor plans, rooflines, entry and context. Passes only if block substitution alone cannot explain the difference.

### Drill B: quiet-vs-busy revision
Build or depict a detailed wall and remove at least a third of its tiny nonstructural details *as a diagnostic exercise*. Evaluate whether main volume and structural rhythm become clearer. Removal is not an invariant quota; restore details that were actually necessary.

### Drill C: cliff mass then texture
First create a 30-block-ish stretch of rock ridge with a legible silhouette using only one stone family. Only if the form passes, introduce a few context-specific complementary blocks, talus and vegetation. Do not assert quality if no screenshot is available.

### Drill D: tree individuality
Design three trees from the same species at distinct growth stages, rooted at different topographic locations. Compare branch silhouettes, canopy breaks and relationship to the path; none should be a rescaled copy.

### Drill E: fix the worst angle
Look at the least attractive side elevation. Correct its underlying wall/roof/terrain composition *without adding dozens of decorations*. Capture the same view afterwards.

### Drill F: survival practicality
Build a designed house so tools, doors, stairs, beds and workstations function in-game under the user's stated constraints. If a visual change hurts navigation, it is not an unqualified improvement.

## How to handle failures

- If the image is **ugly but technically valid**, do not hide behind passing compilation/tests; rework architecture.
- If the agent has **no screenshot tool**, do not invent observations; provide coordinate plans and request/identify a viable inspection path.
- If game placement failed, diagnose version/API/permission/data issues and revalidate rather than calling it an artistic choice.
- If a material palette is too busy, reconstruct a quiet comparison board before texturing again.
- If scenery is boring, change terrain silhouette and routes before adding flowers.

## Acceptance criteria

A truly finished, tool-enabled commission has:
- explicit design/site/palette reasoning;
- actual authored construction in target artifact;
- screenshots or direct views from every important angle;
- corrections prompted by at least one grounded review issue **if an issue exists**;
- working entrance/paths and required interactions;
- palette and terrain integrated, not independent decorations;
- honest validation evidence, with unresolved limits surfaced.

These are *practice standards*, not a claim the skill itself guarantees a certain beauty score.
