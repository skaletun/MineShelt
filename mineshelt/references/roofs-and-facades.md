# Roofs, facades and truthful detail

Roofs are often the largest visible surface in Minecraft. They determine the building's era, climate response, height and visual identity. Designing a facade without resolving its roof usually causes fake overhangs, giant empty lofts and arbitrary ornamentation.

## Choose roof by function and context

- **Gable**: efficient directionality; suited to narrow halls, workshops, cottages and long buildings. Vary pitch and end-wall treatment according to context.
- **Hip**: calmer silhouette with weather protection on multiple sides; good for freestanding substantial houses and certain civic forms.
- **Shed / lean-to**: honest roof for additions, fuel bays and utility spaces. Its high side should clearly attach to a supporting volume.
- **Multi-gable**: justified where interior wings intersect. Check valleys for water management and awkward block seams.
- **Flat/parapet**: viable for dry climates, fortifications and specific styles; deliberate drainage still matters.
- **Vaulted, domed or tower cap**: earned by ceremonial, defensive or monumental programs; model support and transitions, not just an impressive crown.

Do not use a dramatic Nordic gable by default for every biome.

## Work in three dimensions

Before choosing stair/slab combinations, sketch cross-sections. Define span, spring line, apex, overhang, wall-to-roof junction, ridge direction and thickness. Revisit the hero view: too-tall roofs can swallow the building; too-flat roofs can make it read as a crate.

Use block states intentionally. A stair should turn the profile cleanly; an upside-down stair or slab should close a joint rather than exist as a decorative accident. Inspect corners and ridges from diagonal angles to catch missing stairs, exposed backs and jagged seams.

### Eaves

Eaves protect and shadow walls. Supports may be brackets, continuous beams or a hidden structural approach appropriate to style. Avoid huge roof projections supported by a lone fence post. In snowy biomes, roof edges and porch thresholds need plausible shedding or protection.

### Intersections

For crossing roof volumes, verify:
- which ridge is higher;
- where each plane terminates;
- whether valleys leak visually into open voids;
- whether wall tops protrude through roof slopes;
- whether an annex has a believable flashing/joint transition.

Complex intersections can be intentionally rough in an improvised shack, but they cannot be left broken by accident.

## Window composition

Window positions follow plan and daylight. Choose size and rhythm by room use: workspace needs light near a bench, storeroom uses small protected openings, great hall wants strong hierarchy, tower may use narrow slits for defensive reasons.

For each elevation draw:
- major solids and window voids;
- posts or masonry piers;
- the entrance threshold;
- under-eave or parapet termination;
- secondary projections, if truly useful.

Align bays when the construction system suggests alignment. Break them for a stated function, not because asymmetry itself is a target.

## Doors and entrances

Entrance is a sequence, not one placed door: path → grade transition → sheltered threshold → readable portal → first interior space. The most prominent entrance should correspond to the functional public approach unless the brief asks for secrecy or defense.

Do not place a decorative door in front of an inaccessible wall. Check hinge/facing, collision, stair height, nearby lanterns and wet-weather usability.

## Ornament as construction language

Strong detail often sits at **transitions**:
- change in foundation material at a sill or retaining wall;
- real beam head seated on a post;
- smoke outlet visually continuous with the hearth;
- brackets beneath an overhang;
- shutters attached to an opening;
- a sign supported where a passerby would read it;
- a worn step or repaired corner where people pass.

Avoid “detail spraying”: rows of buttons along every stone course; trapdoors on every window; lanterns every third block; decorative chains disconnected from weight or purpose.

## Facade depth

Depth can come from actual structure: recessed infill, projecting beam frame, shutter thickness, porches, covered galleries, arcade, bay windows. Don't inflate every wall with several nested layers unless building type and scale justify it. Distinguish profile-making depth from ornamental protrusion.

## Revision guide

When a roof looks wrong, check in this order:
1. Is pitch compatible with width, climate and massing?
2. Does apex/ridge support the composition?
3. Are wall-to-roof joints structurally readable?
4. Do lower annex roofs actually connect?
5. Are stair/slab states correctly oriented?
6. Are eaves and ornament excessive?

Only add decorative variants after the first five pass.
